# From proposal to agreed need

Use this section sequence for an idea brief:
Frame; Actors; Problem; Smallest valuable outcome; Non-goals; Ranked questions;
Assumptions; Risks; Proposed FEAT split; Design-leakage self-check.

Frame states who needs what result and in which situation, in one sentence. Actors
name a role, its operating context and current tool. Problem describes an observed
obstacle, its frequency or cost, and the evidence source. Outcome describes a useful
change with a way to observe it, not a screen or integration. Unknown measurements
stay unknown until confirmed; never turn an attractive estimate into a baseline.

For refunds, a solution-led statement might demand a prominent coloured control
connected to a payment service. Instead, describe the inability to request a refund
without support and the recorded delay. The smallest outcome might be that a customer
can lodge a request and obtain its calculated amount pending approval, without asking
support to enter it. This leaves interface and implementation choices open.

List at least three non-goals with reasons. For example: item-level refunds require a
separate allocation rule; digital purchases lack delivery evidence; automatic payout
bypasses the finance approval boundary. These are candidate boundaries, not universal
policy. Confirm them against the actual sources.

Make an assumption refutable: “We believe support enters most refund requests;
false if a sample of 50 cases contains fewer than 26 support-entered cases by the
agreed review date.” Write a date only when agreed. A risk needs an owner, impact,
likelihood and response, not merely an alarming label.

Choose elicitation by evidence gap. Document analysis extracts existing rules;
interviews explain conflicting interpretations; observation reveals actual work;
a workshop resolves a boundary across roles; a small prototype explores a solution
only after the problem is agreed. These are practical technique choices, not a
reproduction of a methodology catalogue.

In synthesis mode, map pasted statements into sections, attach provenance, and
ask only about contradictions or missing decisions. When sources disagree, keep
both claims and open a Q record; recency alone does not establish policy authority.
